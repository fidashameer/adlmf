H5  Pillar 1 lock-order prediction, 20 trials per setting

  (a) Lead time against contention. Reversed-order services are introduced
      at tick 400 into an already-running system.
      heavy       deadlocks/run    916 | lead   -474.1 +/- 242.2 ticks | arrives TOO LATE - the deadlock happens first
      moderate    deadlocks/run    508 | lead    +14.5 +/- 8.1 ticks | warns BEFORE the deadlock
      light       deadlocks/run     32 | lead   +669.0 +/- 161.3 ticks | warns BEFORE the deadlock
      very light  deadlocks/run      6 | lead  +2144.5 +/- 406.7 ticks | warns BEFORE the deadlock
      Why: the check needs a service to SUCCESSFULLY complete an out-of-order
      acquisition before it has any evidence. Under heavy contention that
      service deadlocks on its first attempt, so the deadlock beats the warning.

  (b) Robustness to delayed and lost observations, at moderate contention.
      clean             precision 1.000 | attribution 1.000 | recall 1.000 +/- 0.000 | lead +21.4
      delay=3           precision 1.000 | attribution 1.000 | recall 0.988 +/- 0.024 | lead +2.4
      delay=3 loss=5%   precision 1.000 | attribution 1.000 | recall 0.887 +/- 0.075 | lead +12.8
      delay=8 loss=15%  precision 1.000 | attribution 1.000 | recall 0.725 +/- 0.112 | lead +4.5
      precision   = the flagged lock pair really is a dangerous pair
      attribution = the two services the flag NAMED really do disagree on order.
                    Tracked separately because a flag can name the right pair
                    for the wrong reason; an earlier version of the gap-skipping
                    logic did exactly that and precision alone did not catch it.

H7  Ablation of Pillar 2, 20 trials per cell
    'disrupted' is the policy's own objective; 'completed' is independent.

    12 services:
      cost-only (prior art)      completed    199 +/- 6      disrupted  1.970 +/- 0.020
      blast only, fixed w        completed    230 +/- 29     disrupted  1.820 +/- 0.015
      blast + self-calibration   completed    230 +/- 29     disrupted  1.820 +/- 0.015
      blast + fairness           completed    450 +/- 4      disrupted  2.256 +/- 0.010
      full (all three)           completed    434 +/- 5      disrupted  2.384 +/- 0.010

    30 services:
      cost-only (prior art)      completed    113 +/- 3      disrupted  6.877 +/- 0.187
      blast only, fixed w        completed      2 +/- 1      disrupted  4.712 +/- 0.005
      blast + self-calibration   completed      2 +/- 1      disrupted  4.712 +/- 0.005
      blast + fairness           completed    348 +/- 2      disrupted  6.695 +/- 0.107
      full (all three)           completed    332 +/- 2      disrupted  6.761 +/- 0.123

    60 services:
      cost-only (prior art)      completed    111 +/- 3      disrupted 11.976 +/- 0.192
      blast only, fixed w        completed      1 +/- 0      disrupted  9.760 +/- 0.098
      blast + self-calibration   completed      1 +/- 0      disrupted  9.760 +/- 0.098
      blast + fairness           completed    346 +/- 3      disrupted 11.769 +/- 0.120
      full (all three)           completed    335 +/- 3      disrupted 11.858 +/- 0.152

    Reading:
    - blast radius alone starves the cheapest victims. At 30 and 60 services
      it collapses throughput to almost nothing while posting the BEST
      disruption figure, which is the clearest illustration in this suite of
      why a policy must not be judged on the metric it optimises.
    - the fairness term is what makes the approach beat the prior art on an
      independent metric: roughly three times cost-only's completed count at
      30 and 60 services, at comparable disruption.
    - self-calibration does not earn its place. In the arms without fairness
      it is inert (identical to fixed weights) because those arms complete so
      little that almost no feedback arrives. In the arms with fairness it is
      actively worse on throughput. H9 pins down why, so the framework ships
      with it switched off (recovery.DefaultAlpha = 0).

H8  Network partition, 20 trials
    One service is cut off at tick 1000 for 1500 ticks: still running and
    still holding its locks, but unreachable and silent. All rows are the
    adaptive policy with one mechanism toggled, except the last.

                                                   episodes   futile attempts    completed txns
      no partition handling                        22.90         46.2 +/- 33.8       876 +/- 46
      weighted term only (as the report states it)  0.00          0.0 +/- 0.0        930 +/- 16
      tier preference only, weight zero             0.00          0.0 +/- 0.0        930 +/- 16
      both (shipped configuration)                  0.00          0.0 +/- 0.0        930 +/- 16
      cost-only (context only: differs in more)    30.15        164.4 +/- 56.0      1062 +/- 88

    Reading this honestly:
    - "futile attempts" is inflated by re-detection. The monitor re-examines
      the graph every 5 ticks, so one deadlock that never clears contributes
      hundreds of attempts. "Episodes" is the number of distinct times the
      engine started aiming at a service that could not receive the order.
    - the weighted term and the tier both reduce futility to zero here, so
      this run does not distinguish them. A separate measurement
      (TestPartitionMargin) shows why: when both a reachable and an
      unreachable candidate are present, the cost gap between them is near
      zero, so a weight of 2.0 dominates easily. That is a property of this
      workload, not of the formula: the weighted term is a fixed number
      competing with unbounded costs, so a workload with larger cost spreads
      would overrule it. The tier does not depend on any margin.
    - within the controlled rows, partition handling also raises throughput
      (first row versus the next three, confidence intervals not overlapping),
      because the deadlock clears instead of standing until the lease lapses.
    - the cost-only row completes MORE than any of them. That is not evidence
      against partition handling: cost-only also lacks the fairness term, and
      a futile abort destroys no work, so it both stalls more and wastes less.
      It is listed to show why the cross-policy comparison was dropped.

H10 Deadlock frequency against the baselines, 20 trials
    Objective 3 names three comparison metrics. This is the third.

    low contention (lock pool 12):
      timeout (baseline 1)     deadlocks    288   completed  1500   per 1k completed   192.3 +/- 4.6  
      cost-only (baseline 2)   deadlocks    644   completed  3454   per 1k completed   186.6 +/- 3.3  
      youngest (baseline 3)    deadlocks    648   completed  3406   per 1k completed   190.5 +/- 3.2  
      adaptive (ours)          deadlocks    629   completed  3536   per 1k completed   178.0 +/- 2.8  

    medium contention (lock pool 6):
      timeout (baseline 1)     deadlocks    318   completed   263   per 1k completed  1212.0 +/- 19.1 
      cost-only (baseline 2)   deadlocks    709   completed   192   per 1k completed  3713.9 +/- 139.8
      youngest (baseline 3)    deadlocks    710   completed   170   per 1k completed  4200.5 +/- 158.5
      adaptive (ours)          deadlocks    714   completed   450   per 1k completed  1585.0 +/- 20.5 

    high contention (lock pool 3):
      timeout (baseline 1)     deadlocks    224   completed   108   per 1k completed  2067.9 +/- 15.5 
      cost-only (baseline 2)   deadlocks    799   completed     1   per 1k completed 655410.3 +/- 123648.5
      youngest (baseline 3)    deadlocks    799   completed     1   per 1k completed 687888.9 +/- 115237.2
      adaptive (ours)          deadlocks    468   completed   332   per 1k completed  1406.6 +/- 17.3 

    Read the last column, not the first. A policy that leaves deadlocks
    standing records fewer of them, because the services involved never get
    back to work and so never collide again -- the timeout rows show exactly
    this. Deadlocks per thousand completed transactions is how much deadlock
    the system suffers per unit of work it actually finishes.
    Even that column does not fully rescue timeout: at medium contention it
    posts a better ratio than ours while completing about 40% fewer
    transactions, because throttling the system is a way of avoiding
    deadlock that no one would choose. Read the two columns together.
    The youngest-transaction baseline lands almost exactly on cost-only and
    collapses with it at high contention (1 completed transaction, hence the
    absurd ratio). Both starve the same victims. That the textbook rule a
    practitioner would reach for first fails the same way is a better
    argument for the fairness term than any comparison against cost-only.
